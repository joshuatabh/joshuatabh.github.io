            <h3>Environmental effects overtake selection to shape avian body size.</h3>
            <p> The average body sizes of many terrestrial species have been declining as our climate has warmed. Why and how these declines are occurring is not yet known. By using historic and contemporary capture records from across North America, we find that environmental effects are becoming increasingly important determinants of size in birds, probably because of climate-related changes in resource abundance.</p>
            <p><b>J. Tabh, P. Nyquist, A. Nord.</b></p>

            <h3>Shrinking body size may not ease heat balance in a warmer world</h3>
            <p>Classical theory posits that animal body sizes should be smaller in warmer environments than colder environments, predominantly because a smaller body size should reduce the energetic costs of heat dissipation. This theory has been used to explain why the body sizes of many terrestrial animals have decreased in recent decades. Here, we argue that this logic may be too simplistic, particularly since it ignores other evolved mechanisms to shed excess body heat. Using cross-species modelling, we show that heat dissipation is not meaningfully eased when body size declines. </p>
            <p><b>A. Nord<sup>‡</sup>, E. Persson<sup>‡</sup>, J. Tabh<sup>‡</sup>, E. Thoral<sup>‡</sup>.</b></p>

            <h3>Plasticity explains some effects of climate on size, but not always adaptively.</h3>
            <p>Bergmann's Rule and Allen's Rule state that animals living in the warmth tend to be smaller and  longer-limbed than those living in the cold. These morphological patterns are generally understood as adaptive, namely because they *should* make body temperature regulation energetically cheaper. In this paper, we review over century of experimental studies to show that Bergmann's and Allen's Rules can emerge within a generation, and that their emergence might not always be for adaptive reasons. A theoretical framework explaining this phenomenon is presented. </p>
            <p><b>J. Tabh, A. Nord.</b></p>

            <h3>Parental thermal environments influence young brook trout.</h3>
            <p>Climate change threatens cold-adapted species that struggle to cope with rising temperatures. We asked whether brook trout — a cold-adapted fish — could adjust their heat tolerance within a single generation, or pass that adjustment on to their offspring. Although both types of flexibility exist, they are modest at best, and offspring from heat-exposed parents were in poorer condition overall. To our surprise, fathers had a stronger influence on offspring thermal responses than mothers.</p>

            <h3>The right hormonal patterns to ovulate in chameleons. </h3>
            <p>When considering reproduction, having high levels of serum glucocorticoids (often called "stress hormones") is not what most would consider good. Yet evidence from many animal species suggests that levels of these hormones are often high in the breeding season; higher than during non-breeding times in fact. By categorising hormonal profiles of veiled chameleon females, we show that glucocorticoid levels tend to be higher during cycles that include ovulation than those that don't. Why remains unclear. </p>

            <h3>Evidence for a trade-off between the stress response and body temperature regulation.</h3>
            <p>Warm-blooded animals commonly redistribute their blood throughout their bodies when exposed to a stressor. A consequence of this redistribution is a major shift in temperature across bodily tissues. Using the black-capped chickadee as a model species, we show that this redistribution may be part of an adaptive response to reduce costs of body temperature regulation when energy is instead needed to cope with the stressor at hand.</p>

            <h3>Sharing a nest has its costs.</h3>
            <p>Most birds raise their young in nests tended by a solitary pair. However, for a select few species, raising young is a community affair. Using the smooth-billed ani as a model species, we show that sharing a nest between multiple pairs can carry a hidden cost. As group size increases, so too do markers of energetic demand (here, average levels of the glucocorticoid hormone corticosterone). This raises an interesting question — why share at all? </p>

            <h3>Regulators of Wnt signalling might be more stable than we thought.</h3>
            <p>Previous research showed that Dishevelled, a protein involved in regulating a critical developmental pathway called Wnt signalling, might be the target of other proteins involved in curbing that signalling when it's already underway (the Nkd proteins). In this study, we show that this is not always the case, and that for the most part, Dishevelled remains stable in the presence of Nkds.</p>

            <h3>Robustness: unpacking priorities and possible futures.</h3>
            <p>Climate swings can be challenging to sustain, particularly when they occur in the middle of a high intensity breeding season. In <a href="https://www.cell.com/current-biology/abstract/S0960-9822(26)00313-1">a recent study</a>, Taff et al show that how female tree swallows respond to cold snaps is variable, with some females provisioning their young with surprising consistency despite the physiological challenges that that cold presents. In this dispatch, I unpack what that consistency means for fitness, and how Taff et al's broader findings can inform us about priorities and trade-offs in variable climates. </p>
            <p><b>J. Tabh.</b></p>
