<p class="research-intro dropcap">Life has existed on Earth for more than 3 billion years, enduring extreme glaciation, volcanic eruptions, oxygen depressions, and enormous climatic swings. Such miraculous resilience is only possible because of adaptive evolution.</p>
<p class="research-intro"> In many ways, it might seem that evolution is capable of overcoming any barrier that might challenge the continuation of living organisms. A direct corollary of this idea is that evolution itself is boundless. But if that were true, why is the diversity of life on Earth limited in the way that it is? Why, for example, are the ouroboros and <a href="">Pandoran fauna</a> relegated to mythology, and true organismal forms and functions are far narrower than what we can imagine? </p>
<p class="research-intro"> I investigate how Earth's physical forces guide and limit evolutionary possibility. These days, I primarily use body size as a focal trait, allowing the influences of certain physical factors on evolution to be tested in concrete ways. Below are short descriptions of targeted projects that are currently underway. </p>

      <h4><b>Constraints of Temperature on Animal Size</b></h4>
      <p> Animals that live in cold environments tend to be larger than those that live in warm environments. This phenomenon — commonly known as "Bergmann's Rule" — has been well known for almost 200 years. Yet how and why it occurs remains an unsolved mystery. I am developing explicit mathematical models that combine thermodynamics with animal energetics to test whether Bergmann's Rule emerges to allow energy intake and energy use (both of which are shaped by environmental temperature) to be in a state of equilibrium. Support for this hypothesis would identify temperature as a fundamental constraint on evolution, acting through its direct effects on the energetic functioning of organisms.  </p>

      <h4><b>Inherent Effects of Temperature on Development</b></h4>
      <p>
        Bergmann's Rule (see above) typically refers to correlations between body size and environmental temperature <i>across species</i>. However, a similar phenomenon often occurs <i>within species</i> too. Decades of experimental research have shown that some of this temperature–size relationship is established during development, with individuals reared in the heat reaching reproductive maturity at smaller sizes than those reared in the cold. While this temperature-dependent response may be adaptive and programmed genetically, it is also possible that it merely represents an emergent consequence of how temperature naturally affects rates of chemical, enzymatic, and metabolic reactions. I am testing this alternative possibility using tools from neuroscience and molecular biology.
      </p>

      <h4><b>Role of Gravity in Shaping Animal Size</b></h4>
      <p>
        Gravity affects nearly all aspects of life of Earth, from locomotion and nutrient transport to structural integrity and tissue composition required to sustain that integrity. Perhaps unsurprisingly, the magnitude of these effects directly scales with body size, ultimately limiting the maximal size that a species can attain on our planet. But new measures from advanced satellite missions now tell us that gravity is more uneven across Earth than we once thought — so what does this mean for the size and shapes that life can take in different locations? To answer this question, I am combining satellite data with long-term measurements of animal morphology to test how variations in gravity across the planet relate to current and historic patterns of animal size. </p>
